01 / Hall of Fame

Designs that share power

Promising examples—not perfect solutions—where community knowledge and participation shape how the tool works.

Fame / 01Offline mapping · App

Mapeo

Local-first mapping for Indigenous land defenders

Why it earns a place
Digital Democracy developed Mapeo with Indigenous partners, including Waorani communities, who wanted tools for making and managing their own maps. Its offline, local-first approach helps communities document territory and environmental threats on their terms, rather than depending on an outside platform to define what matters.
Justice check
Maps can expose sacred or vulnerable places. Community consent, secure storage, and the right to keep information private must remain in community hands; offline access alone does not guarantee data sovereignty.

Fame / 02Civic participation · Platform

Decidim

Barcelona’s open-source platform for public participation

Why it earns a place
Residents and community groups can propose ideas, debate public plans, take part in participatory budgets, and follow proposals. The platform connects online and face-to-face participation; its reusable open-source code and collaborative development make the infrastructure more inspectable and adaptable than a closed vendor product.
Justice check
A participation portal does not guarantee representative influence. Language access, disability access, offline options, fair moderation, and visible follow-through from public officials determine whose input changes an outcome.

02 / Hall of Shame

Designs that reproduce harm

These cases show what happens when historical inequality is treated as neutral data or when people subject to a system have little say in it.

Shame / 01Risk assessment · Criminal justice

COMPAS

A proprietary recidivism risk score used in U.S. courts

Why it fails the test
In a Broward County sample, ProPublica found that among defendants not arrested for a new crime within two years, COMPAS classified 44.9% of Black defendants as higher risk, compared with 23.5% of White defendants. The vendor disputed ProPublica’s analysis and emphasized similar predictive accuracy across racial groups; later research showed that accuracy and error-rate parity can conflict. Those choices matter when scores inform decisions about liberty.
Design justice concern
ProPublica reported that the score calculations were proprietary, limiting public scrutiny. When a score can inform decisions about someone’s liberty, the person affected needs a way to understand, challenge, and appeal it—and the system must account for the unequal institutions its data reflects.

Evidence[5][6]

Shame / 02Hiring · Experimental AI tool

Amazon’s résumé-ranking prototype

A hiring tool trained on the company’s past applications

Why it fails the test
Reuters reported that the model learned from a decade of mostly male résumés and downgraded applications mentioning “women’s” and graduates of two all-women’s colleges. Treating past hiring patterns as a proxy for merit turned existing gender imbalance into a filter for future candidates.
Design justice concern
As designed, candidates would have had little ability to inspect or challenge a hidden ranking; Reuters reported that removing a few gendered terms did not guarantee other discriminatory patterns were gone. Fairer design would define criteria with affected candidates, test for disparate outcomes before deployment, and provide a transparent human review and appeal path.

Scope: Amazon said the prototype was never used by recruiters to evaluate candidates; Reuters sources said recruiters reviewed its recommendations but did not rely on them alone. This is a design failure in an experiment, not a claim that the tool made final hiring decisions.

Evidence[7]
